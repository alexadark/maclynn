Choosing a note-taker

How we'll capture sales meetings, in person and online, and why we recommend Jamie. Prepared by Alexandra Spalato for Rachel, Alexa and Tori.

Every proposal starts with a conversation. Today, most of that conversation lives in Tori's notebook. She photographs the pages, types them up with ChatGPT, and then writes the proposal. It works, but it eats evenings, and some meetings never get written up at all.

The new sales workflow changes the first step. With the client's permission, the meeting is recorded and transcribed. Claude turns the transcript into notes in Tori's own format, files them in GoHighLevel, and drafts the proposal. Tori still writes her personal impressions by hand. The machine takes the dictation, she keeps the judgement.

For that to work, we need one tool that listens well, keeps the data safe, and hands everything to Claude. This page explains which one, and why.

What the tool has to do

Maclynn's meetings aren't ordinary sales calls. People talk about past relationships, family, money, sometimes health. So the bar is higher than "it transcribes well".

  • Work in person. Tori meets most leads face to face, in cafés, hotels and offices. A tool that only joins video calls misses the meetings that matter most.
  • Work online without a bot. A visible "note-taker has joined" robot feels wrong in a first conversation with a high-net-worth client.
  • Keep the data in Europe. UK GDPR applies, and much of what clients share counts as sensitive data.
  • Never train on our data. Guaranteed in writing, for the whole team.
  • Talk to Claude and GoHighLevel. The transcript has to reach Claude without copy and paste, and the notes have to land on the contact in GoHighLevel.
  • Be easy for Tori. One button, on the phone she already carries.

Our recommendation: Jamie

Jamie, Team plan, used on the iPhone. €33 per person per month, billed annually. No device to buy for now.

Website: meetjamie.ai · pricing · security

Jamie is a European note-taker built around privacy, and it's the only tool we found that ticks every box at once.

  • In person: the iPhone app records a face-to-face meeting. Tori places the phone on the table and taps record.
  • Online: it captures Teams, Zoom and Meet calls from the computer, with no bot joining the call.
  • Data: hosted in the EU, with transcripts stored in Frankfurt. The audio is deleted once the transcript is made. ISO 27001 certified.
  • No training: stated on every plan, including Team.
  • Claude: Jamie connects to Claude directly, so Claude can read a meeting the moment it ends.
  • GoHighLevel: an API and webhooks let us send notes to the contact automatically.
  • Consent: it can send the client a consent email before the meeting.
  • Team: shared templates, so every salesperson gets notes in the same house format.

Three things to settle before we sign

  • The data processing agreement. Jamie lists it under its Enterprise plan. We'll ask for it on the Team plan, since we handle sensitive personal data.
  • British English. No independent test proves how well it handles British accents in a noisy room. Tori will try it on two real meetings first.
  • Where the summaries are written. Jamie uses AI models from Anthropic and OpenAI to write its notes. We'll get written confirmation that this processing stays in the EU or UK.

Why not the others

  • Granola is the nicest to use and the cheapest. It's our runner-up. But it stores data in the United States, and switching off AI training for the whole team needs its Enterprise plan. For conversations this personal, that's a step too far.
  • Plaud is excellent hardware. Its software would become a second system alongside Claude, and we'd rather keep one.
  • Fireflies is already paid for, on five seats. But it joins calls as a bot, and its transcripts never became part of the routine. We'd cancel it once Jamie is running.
  • Fathom is built for video calls. It joins as a bot and keeps data in the United States.
  • Teams Copilot only works inside Teams meetings. It can't help Tori in a café.

Do we need a device?

Not to begin with. For two people at a table, an iPhone lying flat between them records clearly enough in most rooms. It's discreet, everyone already has one, and everything stays in one app.

If the first trial in a busy café isn't clear enough, the answer is the Plaud Note Pro, about £169. It's the size of a credit card and picks up voices up to five metres away. It looks like an accessory, not equipment, which matters in a luxury setting. The catch is a second app to manage, so we'd only add it if the phone falls short.

Asking for consent

Recording a client is fine under UK GDPR as long as they know and agree. Because Maclynn's conversations often touch on health and private life, which count as special category data, a little extra care is needed.

  • Ask at the start of every meeting, in plain words. Something like:

"Would you mind if I record our conversation? It's only so I can prepare an accurate proposal for you. It stays within Maclynn, and you can ask us to delete it at any time."

  • If the answer is no, Tori takes notes by hand as she does today. Nothing else changes.
  • Mention it when the meeting is booked, through Jamie's consent email or a line in the GoHighLevel booking form.
  • Update the privacy notice to cover meeting recordings.
  • Decide how long transcripts are kept, then delete them on schedule.
  • Write a short data protection impact assessment before the first recording. The ICO expects one when sensitive data is processed this way. I'll draft it for review.
